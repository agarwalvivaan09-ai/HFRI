How big a financial shock can a household absorb?

The Household Financial Resilience Index (HFRI) turns savings, debt, emergency cash and spending stability into a single score from 0 to 100. Version 2 has been tested on more than 12,000 real families from the U.S. Federal Reserve's Survey of Consumer Finances, and its weights and score bands now come from that evidence. Every formula is published on this page, and you can test it on your own numbers.

The project

Version 1 built the index on simulated households. Version 2 tests it on real ones.

Does the HFRI correctly identify real families who are in financial trouble today, and real families who will get into trouble in the future? And if its original weights are not the best ones, what should they be?

Income alone does not show how well a household can cope with a bad month. Two families with the same salary can be in very different positions if one holds several months of cash and little debt, while the other has almost no buffer and heavy loan repayments. HFRI treats resilience as the capacity to absorb a financial shock while still meeting essential obligations, and measures it across four dimensions: Savings Strength, Debt Sustainability, Emergency Coverage and Expense Stability.

Version 1 built the scoring curves and tested the index on 1,000 computer-generated households, with each dimension counting for a quarter of the score. Version 2 checks it against real financial trouble (missed loan payments, payday loans, bankruptcy and refused credit) reported by families in three Federal Reserve surveys, including a panel that interviewed the same families before and after the 2008 financial crisis.

The original index worked: among families with the lowest tenth of scores, about one in five were badly behind on a loan payment, and among the highest tenth almost none were. But the four parts should not count equally. Cash savings explain who is in trouble now, while debt explains who will get into trouble later. A good index needs both.

How the score is built

Five indicators, each turned into a 0 to 100 score by a curve fitted to published landmarks, then weighted using real-world evidence.

HFRI = 0.05·S + 0.30·D + 0.55·E + 0.10·X

D = 0.40·DDTI + 0.60·DDSTI

S savings, D debt, E emergency coverage, X expense stability. Higher is always more resilient. Version 1 used equal weights of 0.25. Version 2 tested all 1,770 combinations in 5% steps against real distress and chose 5/30/55/10: among the top three, and almost exactly the average of the weights picked in ten held-out tests (3.5 / 30.5 / 56 / 10).

Savings Strength

SR = (Y − C) / Y

S = 100·(1 − e−5.89681·(SR + 0.10))  for SR > −0.10, otherwise 0

Rewards a surplus, with diminishing returns as the savings rate rises. Y is annual disposable income and C is annual spending.

Emergency Coverage

EC = Liquid savings / Monthly essential spending

E = 100·(1 − e−0.37028·EC0.901391)

Counts only money that can be reached quickly. The first few months of cover add the most protection, and later months add less. Three months is a common benchmark in the Federal Reserve literature, used here as a landmark and not a pass mark.

Expense Stability

CV = Standard deviation of monthly spending / Mean monthly spending

X = 100·e−3.13957·CV1.30837

Spending that swings a lot is harder to plan around. The main landmark, CV near 0.33, comes from JPMorgan Chase Institute evidence on spending volatility. This is the most exploratory dimension, because lumpy spending is not always a sign of weakness.

      What HFRI is not

      Version 2 is tested on real families, but there are still clear limits to what a score can tell you.

      • The evidence is American. The weights and bands come from U.S. families. HFRI uses ratios, so it can be applied to rupee figures, but the weights have not yet been tested on Indian household data.
      • Some survey measures are stand-ins. Spending stability in 2025 and the saving habit in 2007 come from simple survey questions, not month-by-month records, and 2007 essential spending is assumed to be 70% of income. Better data could change the weights on these parts.
      • One crisis. The "trouble later" test comes from 2007–09, a crisis driven by job losses and debt. A different kind of shock, such as fast-rising prices, could shift the balance between savings and debt.
      • A small trade-off. On unseen families, the new weights are slightly worse than the original at predicting future trouble (by about 0.01 AUC). The calculator shows the Version 1 score alongside for that reason.
      • Prediction, not cause. A low score predicts trouble. It does not prove that raising the score would prevent it.
      • The cash-flow stress test is an extra. It is a simple illustration added to this calculator and is not part of either paper.
      • It is not financial advice. A score of 70 is not a promise that a household is safe, and a score of 30 is not a verdict that it will fail.

      The next step in the research is to test the revised HFRI on Indian household data.
