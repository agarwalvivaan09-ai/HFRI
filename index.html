How big a financial shock can a household absorb?

The Household Financial Resilience Index (HFRI) turns savings, debt, emergency cash and spending stability into a single score from 0 to 100. Every formula, anchor point and assumption behind the score is published on this page, and you can test it on your own numbers.

The project

A transparent index, tested on a simulated population.

Can a transparent mathematical index combining savings, debt, emergency liquidity, and expenditure stability provide a meaningful measure of household financial resilience?

Income alone does not show how well a household can cope with a bad month. Two families with the same salary can be in very different positions if one holds several months of cash and little debt, while the other has almost no buffer and heavy loan repayments. HFRI treats resilience as the capacity to absorb a financial shock while still meeting essential obligations, and measures it across four dimensions: Savings Strength, Debt Sustainability, Emergency Coverage and Expense Stability.

The research had two phases. Phase I built the scoring functions, checked how the index behaves internally and tested how much the rankings depend on the weights. Phase II compared the index with an ONS-style liquidity benchmark and put the simulated households through an income shock and a spending shock.

At a glance

Population
1,000 simulated households observed for 12 months: 12,000 household-month observations.
Score range
16.27 to 97.24, with a median of 82.04 and a mean of 75.84.
Weights
25% on each of the four dimensions. Inside Debt Sustainability, 40% on debt-to-income and 60% on debt-service-to-income.
Weight sensitivity
Each dimension was set anywhere from 10% to 40%. The lowest rank correlation with the baseline was 0.9707.
Shock test
75.9% of households could cover a 25% income fall for three months from liquid savings. HFRI correlated 0.8729 with that outcome.
Reproducibility
Python, run in Google Colab, with a fixed random seed of 42.

How the score is built

Five indicators, each turned into a 0 to 100 score by a function fitted to published landmarks.

HFRI = 0.25·S + 0.25·D + 0.25·E + 0.25·X

D = 0.40·DDTI + 0.60·DDSTI

S savings, D debt, E emergency coverage, X expense stability. Higher is always more resilient. Equal weights are a transparent baseline, not a claim that each dimension matters equally in every household.

Savings Strength

SR = (Y − C) / Y

S = 100·(1 − e−5.89681·(SR + 0.10))  for SR > −0.10, otherwise 0

Rewards a surplus, with diminishing returns as the savings rate rises. Y is annual disposable income and C is annual spending.

Two indicators because debt hurts in two ways: the size of the stock (DTI) and the repayment burden today (DSTI). Repayments get the larger weight because they hit monthly cash flow directly.

Emergency Coverage

EC = Liquid savings / Monthly essential spending

E = 100·(1 − e−0.37028·EC0.901391)

Counts only money that can be reached quickly. The first few months of cover add the most protection, and later months add less. Three months is a common benchmark in the Federal Reserve literature, used here as a landmark and not a pass mark.

Expense Stability

CV = Standard deviation of monthly spending / Mean monthly spending

X = 100·e−3.13957·CV1.30837

Spending that swings a lot is harder to plan around. The main landmark, CV near 0.33, comes from JPMorgan Chase Institute evidence on spending volatility. This is the most exploratory dimension, because lumpy spending is not always a sign of weakness.

      What HFRI is not

      The paper's own conclusion is that HFRI is coherent and transparent, not yet validated.

      • The households are simulated. The 1,000 households are artificial and are not a representative sample of Indian families. The score distribution above describes the model, not a population.
      • The anchors and weights are researcher-defined. They are informed by central bank and survey literature, but they were not estimated from observed financial outcomes.
      • The shock test is partly circular. Liquid savings feed both the Emergency Coverage score and the shock-survival check, so a strong link between HFRI and survival is partly built in.
      • Expense Stability is exploratory. A volatile budget is not always a weak one.
      • This calculator goes beyond the paper. The paper tested the formulas on simulated households. Using them on a real household's numbers is an extension that has not been tested against real outcomes, and the cash-flow stress test shown above is an addition that is not in the paper.
      • It is not financial advice. A score is a way to compare financial positions. A score of 70 is not a verdict that a household is safe, and a score of 40 is not a verdict that it is not.

      The next step in the research is to test HFRI against real household data and see whether low scores go with missed payments, borrowing and forced asset sales.
